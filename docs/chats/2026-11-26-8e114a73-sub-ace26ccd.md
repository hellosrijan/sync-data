610	<section class="section reveal" id="background-and-scenario"><div class="wrap"><header class="section-head"><div class="kicker"><i class="ph ph-buildings" aria-hidden="true"></i></div><h2 class="heading-2">Background and scenario</h2></header><div class="section-body"><p class="body-3 measure">A client runs its sales team in Salesforce, a customer relationship management (CRM) product. Tequity has built the client an operations portal. Finance and customer-success staff work in the portal.</p><p class="body-3 measure">The same people exist in both places, as customers and as contacts. A sales person creates a customer in Salesforce. The portal should show that customer. A finance person on the portal then corrects the billing city. Salesforce should show the new city. Later, a sales person changes the phone number in Salesforce. The portal should show the new phone, and the city the finance person set should still be there.</p><p class="body-3 measure">The client also needs the history. They need to know who changed the phone, and who changed the city. The fact of an earlier edit stays on record after a later edit.</p><p class="body-3 measure">Salesforce stores its own copy. The portal stores its copy in PostgreSQL (Postgres). The two copies have to stay in sync. We are designing and building our side. Salesforce already exists, and it stays as it is.</p></div></div></section>
611	<section class="section reveal" id="the-systems-involved"><div class="wrap"><header class="section-head"><div class="kicker"><i class="ph ph-share-network" aria-hidden="true"></i></div><h2 class="heading-2">The systems involved</h2></header><div class="section-body"><p class="body-3 measure">Three parties matter.</p><ol class="cards cols-3 wide"><li><div class="body-3"><strong>Salesforce.</strong> The client's CRM. Sales users edit records here. Salesforce has its own database.</div></li><li><div class="body-3"><strong>The portal.</strong> Our application. Finance and customer-success users edit records here. Our database is Postgres.</div></li><li><div class="body-3"><strong>The network between them.</strong> Salesforce and the portal talk over the public internet, using Hypertext Transfer Protocol Secure (HTTPS). Each side keeps its own database. HTTPS is the only path between them.</div></li></ol><p class="title-3 measure">Who calls whom:</p><ul class="list list-ul measure"><li><div>When a record changes in Salesforce, Salesforce calls us. The call is a webhook: an HTTPS request to an endpoint we host. The webhook names the record and describes the change.</div></li><li><div>When a record changes in the portal, we call Salesforce. The call uses Salesforce's official record application programming interface (API): the create and update operations Salesforce already documents. The portal reaches Salesforce through that record API.</div></li><li><div>A sales user talks only to Salesforce. A portal user talks only to the portal. Neither user talks to the other system.</div></li></ul><p class="body-3 measure">The rate limit below applies to the requests we send to Salesforce. Webhooks Salesforce sends to us sit outside that cap.</p><div class="frame frame-diagram measure"><pre><code> Sales user                         Finance / customer-success user
612	     |                                          |
613	     | uses                                     | uses
614	     v                                          v
615	 Salesforce CRM                           Tequity portal
616	 (their app, their database)              (our app)
617	     |                                          |
618	     |                                          v
619	     |                                     Postgres
620	     |                                     (our database)
621	     |

Say which you choose, and what crosses the boundary between them.</p><p class="body-3 measure">A webhook is a message. Our endpoint has to accept it, check it, and apply it before anything is stored. A call to the Salesforce record API is a request we initiate. Salesforce may accept it, reject it, or be unreachable.</p></div></div></section>
630	<section class="section reveal" id="requirements-and-scale"><div class="wrap"><header class="section-head"><div class="kicker"><i class="ph ph-list-checks" aria-hidden="true"></i></div><h2 class="heading-2">Requirements and scale</h2></header><div class="section-body"><p class="title-3 measure">Records:</p><ul class="list list-ul measure"><li><div>Customers and contacts. About these fields, and no others, for this exercise: customer name, status, and owner; contact full name, phone, email, city, owner, and the customer they belong to.</div></li><li><div><strong>5 million records</strong> in production, customers and contacts counted together.</div></li></ul><p class="title-3 measure">Sync:</p><ul class="list list-ul measure"><li><div>While both systems are up, a create or an update on either side is visible on the other side within <strong>30 seconds</strong>. Thirty seconds is the worst case. Everyday sync should land sooner.</div></li><li><div>Both sides may create. Both sides may update.</div></li><li><div>The current value of a field is the one we can justify. The earlier values, who made them, and when, stay on record. Dropping that history counts as losing a change.</div></li><li><div>An update we send to Salesforce must not bounce back and forth forever.</div></li></ul><p class="title-3 measure">Webhooks:</p><ul class="list list-ul measure"><li><div>Salesforce may deliver the same webhook twice.</div></li><li><div>Salesforce may deliver webhooks in a different order from the order of the edits.</div></li><li><div>Each webhook carries at least the fields in the sample below. <code>occurredAt</code> is the time Salesforce gives the event.</div></li></ul><p class="body-3 measure">The CRM API allows at most <strong>100 requests per second</strong>. That is our cap on calls to Salesforce. We choose how far under the cap to run.</p><p class="body-3 measure">We left the peak edit rate open. State the rate you assume, in edits per second, and use it in the arithmetic.</p><p class="body-3 measure">Sample webhook. This is enough to start. You may add fields. Say why.</p><div class="frame frame-json measure"><pre><code>{

647	<section class="section reveal" id="constraints"><div class="wrap"><header class="section-head"><div class="kicker"><i class="ph ph-barricade" aria-hidden="true"></i></div><h2 class="heading-2">Constraints</h2></header><div class="section-body"><p class="body-3 measure"><strong>Rate limit.</strong> At most 100 requests per second to the Salesforce record API. The cap counts the calls we make, including retries. Webhooks arriving at our endpoint sit outside this cap. If Salesforce tells us to slow down, we slow down, and we keep the work.</p><p class="body-3 measure"><strong>Outages.</strong> Design for the Salesforce API being unreachable for <strong>6 hours</strong>. Webhooks Salesforce tried to send during that window may never arrive. Pending calls to Salesforce must still be there after our own process restarts.</p><p class="body-3 measure">Thirty seconds applies while both systems are up. Catch-up after an outage is a different target. Drain the backlog as fast as the rate limit allows. Compute how long that takes, in minutes, for the edit rate you assumed. Show the division.</p><p class="body-3 measure"><strong>Consistency.</strong> Two people may edit the same record at the same time, on the same side or on opposite sides. Edits to different fields of that record both have to survive. Edits to the same field need one current value and a kept history.</p><p class="body-3 measure">Say what you use to decide which edit is current, on a webhook and on a portal save. Name the field. If you need an API this note does not give you, mark it as an assumption.</p><p class="body-3 measure"><strong>Out of scope for this weekend:</strong> deletes, Salesforce login screens, a visual user interface, a second region, and a load test against real Salesforce. If you write a program, call a fake CRM you write yourself.</p></div></div></section>
648	<section class="section reveal" id="what-you-deliver"><div class="wrap"><header class="section-head"><div class="kicker"><i class="ph ph-files" aria-hidden="true"></i></div><h2 class="heading-2">What you deliver</h2></header><div class="section-body"><p class="body-3 measure">Three things are required. A program is optional.</p><p class="tagline heading-3" data-tagline><span class="word">We</span> <span class="word">ask</span> <span class="word">for</span> <span class="word">the</span> <span class="word">chats</span> <span class="word">and</span> <span class="word">the</span> <span class="word">commits</span> <span class="word">because</span> <span class="word">we</span> <span class="word">want</span> <span class="word">to</span> <span class="word">see</span> <span class="word">how</span> <span class="word">you</span> <span class="word">prompt</span> <span class="word">the</span> <span class="word">artificial</span> <span class="word">intelligence</span> <span class="word">(AI)</span> <span class="word">assistant,</span> <span class="word">and</span> <span class="word">how</span> <span class="word">your</span> <span class="word">thinking</span> <span class="word">develops.</span> <span class="word">The</span> <span class="word">final</span> <span class="word">document</span> <span class="word">alone</span> <span class="word">does</span> <span class="word">not</span> <span class="word">show</span> <span class="word">that.</span></p><div class="panel measure"><div class="panel-body"><h3 class="heading-4">1. The design document</h3><p class="body-3 measure">This is the main piece, a few pages, long enough for the rules and the arithmetic. 119	### 2. Inside the portal
120	
121	Inside the portal box there are three smaller boxes, plus Redis and `apply()` sitting loose.
122	
123	**Django web**, the part that receives requests:
124	- **Webhook endpoint:** Salesforce calls this when a record changes. It validates the request (is it really from Salesforce, is the payload well-formed), then hands it to `apply()`.
125	- **Portal API:** finance users' edits arrive here. It also hands each edit to `apply()`.
126	
127	**`apply(change)`**, the large box in the middle. Every change enters the database through this one function, whether it came from a webhook, a portal user or a catch-up run. It runs in a single database transaction, so either all its writes happen or none do. It's drawn as one box because having one implementation is the point: duplicates, ordering, history and the loop rule are all decided in one place. Its steps are in section 4.

129	**Postgres**, our database. It holds five things:
130	
131	| Table | What it holds | Why it exists |
132	|---|---|---|
133	| **Customer / Contact** | The current value of every field, plus *when* each field was last set, by which side, and **the last value we synced from Salesforce** | The per-field time tells us whether an incoming change is newer. 134	| **field_change** | Every change ever made: field, value, who, when, from which side, and whether it became current | The brief's history requirement. Rows are only added, never changed. |
135	| **change_event** | The ID of every change we've seen, kept 7 days | Duplicate detection: a second copy of an ID is ignored |
136	| **outbox** | Portal edits waiting to be sent to Salesforce, with their state (`pending`, `sent`, `needs_review`), attempts and next retry time | The pending work. It's on disk, so it survives restarts, and it's never dropped. |
137	| **watermark** | How far the last catch-up got | So the next catch-up only asks for what's new |

146	### 3. The three paths
147	
148	#### Path (A): Salesforce → portal (webhook)
149	
150	*Sales user → Salesforce CRM ⇒(A)⇒ webhook endpoint → `apply()` → tables*
151	
152	1. A sales user changes a phone number in Salesforce.
153	2. Salesforce sends a webhook to our endpoint over HTTPS. It carries `eventId`, `recordId`, `occurredAt`, `changedBy` and `changedFields`.
154	3. The endpoint checks the DRF token (401 if wrong), validates the payload (400 if malformed), works out which fields actually changed, and calls `apply()`. Nothing is stored before these checks pass.
155	4. `apply()` updates the current phone (if this change is the newest), writes a history row, and records the event ID.
156	5. **No arrow goes from this path to the outbox.** That's the sync-loop rule: anything that came *from* Salesforce is never sent *back* to Salesforce.
157	
158	It's fast (milliseconds), and it doesn't count against the rate limit.
159	
160	#### Path (B): portal → Salesforce (outbound)
161	
162	*Finance user → Portal API → `apply()` → outbox ··notify··› Redis → Outbound task → rate limiter ⇒(B)⇒ Record API*
163	
164	1. A finance user sets city = Noida.
165	2. The Portal API calls `apply()`, which saves the city, writes a history row, **and in the same transaction** adds an outbox row saying "the city on this record needs sending to Salesforce". Because it's one transaction, we can never end up with the edit saved but the send forgotten, or the reverse.
166	3. The outbox nudges Redis (dotted *notify*), and Redis wakes the outbound task.
167	4. The outbound task claims the pending row (*reads pending*), reads the city's **current** value from the record, and asks the rate limiter for permission. Sending the current value rather than the edit means an older edit can never overwrite a newer one in Salesforce.
168	5. The limiter lets it through, and the task calls the Record API (thick arrow **B**). A create is sent as an upsert carrying our record ID, so a retry can't create a duplicate.
169	6. The dotted **result** arrow is Salesforce's answer:
170	   - **Accepted:** mark the outbox row sent. On a create, also store the new Salesforce ID.
171	   - **Unreachable, timeout or 5xx:** keep the row and retry with backoff (1, 2, 4, 8 s…, never more than 60 s).
172	   - **429 "slow down":** keep the row, and slow the whole limiter down.
173	   - **Rejected for bad data:** move the row to `needs_review` and raise an alert. It's never dropped.
174	
175	   Every retry goes back through the limiter, so retries count toward the cap. Full rules are in [Sending and retries](#sending-and-retries-path-b).
176	
177	This is the only path that writes to Salesforce, and only portal edits feed it (the *portal edits only* label on the arrow into the outbox).
178	
179	#### Path (C): catch-up (portal pulls from Salesforce)
180	
181	*Catch-up task → rate limiter ⇒(C)⇒ Query API ··changed records··› Catch-up task → `apply()` (each record) + watermark*
182	
183	1. It's triggered when Salesforce comes back after an outage, and every 5 minutes as a safety net.
184	2. The task asks the Query API for records changed since the watermark (thick arrow **C**). This call also goes through the limiter.
185	3. Salesforce returns the changed records (dotted *changed records*), up to about 2,000 per call.
186	4. Each record goes through **the same `apply()`** as a webhook (*each record*). So if we'd already received a change by webhook, the copy is recognised and causes nothing new.
187	5. The task moves the watermark forward.
188	
189	This path exists because of one line in the brief: webhooks Salesforce tried to send during the outage "may never arrive". Without (C), sales-side edits made during those 6 hours would be lost for good.
190	
191	### 4. What happens inside `apply()`
192	
193	The box says *dedupe · lock · history · per-field newest wins · outbox*. In order:
194	
195	1. **Dedupe:** try to record the change's ID. If it's already there, this is a duplicate, so reply OK and stop.
196	2. **Lock** the record, so two simultaneous edits to it go one after the other instead of overwriting each other.
197	3. **Echo check:** if the change came from Salesforce and its `changedBy` is *our integration user*, it's our own update coming back. Record it and stop.
198	4. **For each field:**
199	   - always write a history row;
200	   - if the change is newer than that field's stored time, make it the current value;
201	   - if it's older, it stays in history only, marked *superseded*;
202	   - on an exact tie, Salesforce wins.
203	5. **Outbox:** if the change came from the portal and changed at least one field, add an outbox row.
204	6. **Commit**, so all of the above happens or none of it does.

221	  - Portal edits keep working, because path B's first half (`apply()` → outbox) doesn't need Salesforce. Outbox rows pile up safely in Postgres.
222	  - The outbound task's calls fail; the rows stay pending, and a cheap test call every ~10 s checks whether Salesforce is back.
223	  - Webhooks (A) stop arriving.

226	  2. **Then drain the outbox (B)** as fast as the limiter allows. At the assumed 5 portal edits per second: 108,000 pending calls ÷ (90 − 5) per second ≈ **21 minutes**.

237	## `apply(change)`: one function for every inbound change

246	 1. Dedupe     INSERT event_id INTO change_event (unique). Conflict → duplicate: return OK.
247	 2. Lock       SELECT record ... FOR UPDATE   (serialises concurrent edits to one record)
248	 3. Echo       source = salesforce and actor = our integration user → our own update coming
249	               back: record it as a confirmation, apply nothing, stop.
250	 4. Per field  always INSERT field_change(record, field, value, actor, occurred_at, source)
251	               occurred_at > field's stored time → becomes current          (applied)
252	               otherwise → stays in history only                            (superseded)
253	               exact tie → Salesforce wins
254	 5. Outbox     source = portal and a field was applied → INSERT outbox row
255	               source = salesforce → never
256	 6. COMMIT

268	## Sending and retries (path B)
269	
270	Decided ([Design](Design) §6, Q7). The **outbox** drives sending and retries; Dramatiq's own
271	retries are turned off for the outbound task.
272	
273	```
274	pending ──send──► sent ✓
275	   ▲  │
276	   │  ├─ unreachable / timeout / 5xx ─► pending again, next_attempt_at = now + backoff
277	   │  ├─ 429 slow down ───────────────► pending again, at Retry-After; the whole limiter slows
278	   │  └─ 400-type rejection ──────────► needs_review   (never dropped, alert raised)
279	   └── a person fixes it and requeues ◄─┘
280	```
281	
282	| Salesforce's answer | Action |
283	|---|---|
284	| Accepted | `sent`. On a create, store the Salesforce ID. |
285	| Unreachable, timeout, 5xx | Retry at 1, 2, 4, 8 s…, capped at 60 s. Four quick retries take 15 s, inside the 30 s target. |
286	| 429 slow down | Pause the shared limiter until `Retry-After`, halve its rate, then climb back by 5 req/s every 30 s to 90 |
287	| 400-type rejection | `needs_review` and an alert. Never dropped. |
288	| 401 / 403 | Stop all sending and page someone: our credentials are wrong |
289	
290	- **Outage detection:** after 5 consecutive unreachable failures, Salesforce is marked *down*.
291	  Sending stops and a probe runs every 10 s. When it succeeds, catch-up runs, then every pending
292	  row is due at once.
293	- **We send the current value, not the edit.** The worker reads the record's current values for
294	  the changed fields at send time. Pending edits to one record collapse into one call, and an
295	  update sent twice is harmless.
296	- **Workers claim rows** with `SELECT … FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED` and a lease (`lease_until`). If a
297	  worker dies mid-call, the lease runs out and another worker sends the row again.
298	- **Creates are upserts by external ID** (assumption Q13). Our record ID goes with the create, so a
299	  retry after a timeout matches the existing record instead of making a duplicate.
300	- **Wake-up:** Redis nudges the outbound task, and a timer task every 5 s backs up a lost nudge.
301	  Redis holds no work, so its durability no longer matters (Q8 closed).
302	
303	## How the brief's rules are met
304	
305	| Rule | Mechanism |
306	|---|---|
307	| Duplicate webhook | `change_event.event_id` is unique, so a repeat is a no-op. IDs are kept 7 days. |
308	| Out-of-order change | Per-field `occurred_at` comparison. The older change is kept in history but isn't current. |
309	| Different fields edited at once | The comparison is per field, so both edits survive |
310	| Same field edited at once | The newest `occurred_at` is current, and every edit stays in history. Two portal users: last save wins, with no warning (Q4). |
311	| History: who, what, when | `field_change` is written explicitly in `apply()` on every path, including superseded edits and bulk catch-up. It isn't a Django signal, because signals miss superseded edits and bulk writes. |
312	| Sync loop | Only portal edits create outbox rows. Our echo (actor = integration user) is never applied. |
313	| Pending calls survive restarts | The outbox row commits in the same transaction as the edit. Redis is only a wake-up signal, so losing it loses no work. |
314	| Slow down | A 429 pauses the shared limiter until `Retry-After`, halves its rate, then climbs back by 5 req/s every 30 s. The row stays pending. |
315	| Unreachable | Retry with backoff capped at 60 s. After 5 consecutive failures Salesforce is marked down, and a probe checks every 10 s. Nothing is dropped (Q9). |
316	| Rejected (bad data) | `needs_review` plus an alert; a person fixes and requeues it. Never dropped (Q9). |
317	| Webhooks missed in the outage | Catch-up (C), triggered by recovery and every 5 min |
318	
319	## After an outage
320	
321	1. Outbound calls start failing. After 5 consecutive failures Salesforce is marked down: sending
322	   stops, outbox rows stay pending, and a probe checks every 10 s whether Salesforce is back.
323	2. The probe succeeds, so Salesforce is back.
324	3. **Catch-up first.** Pull what changed in Salesforce since the watermark (minus a few minutes
325	   of overlap) and apply each record. Same-field conflicts are settled before we send anything.
326	4. **Then drain the outbox** at the full run rate. Edits to the same record are merged into one
327	   call.

136	Decided (Q7). Mechanism: [Architecture](https://github.com/hellosrijan/sync-data/wiki/Architecture), path B.
137	
138	**The outbox drives sending and retries.** A portal edit that applies at least one field adds an
139	outbox row in the same transaction (§9). Each row has `state`, `attempts`, `next_attempt_at`,
140	`last_error` and `lease_until`.
141	
142	```
143	pending ──send──► sent ✓
144	   ▲  │
145	   │  ├─ unreachable / timeout / 5xx ─► pending again, next_attempt_at = now + backoff
146	   │  ├─ 429 slow down ───────────────► pending again, at Retry-After; the whole limiter slows
147	   │  └─ 400-type rejection ──────────► needs_review   (never dropped, alert raised)
148	   └── a person fixes it and requeues ◄─┘

152	
153	- The Dramatiq outbound task is a single pass over due rows. Redis wakes it, and a timer task
154	  every 5 s backs up a lost wake-up. **Dramatiq's own retries are off for this task**, so no
155	  backoff of hours and no dead-letter set.
156	- Workers claim due rows with `SELECT … FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED` and set `lease_until`. If a worker
157	  dies mid-call, the lease runs out and another worker sends the row again.
158	- **We send the record's current value, not the edit.** The worker reads the current values of
159	  the changed fields at send time. So an older row can't overwrite a newer value in Salesforce,
160	  several pending edits to one record collapse into one call, and an update sent twice is
161	  harmless.

163	  retried after a timeout then matches the existing record instead of making a duplicate. On
164	  success we store the Salesforce ID.

192	  if its `occurred_at` is newer than that field's stored time. Edits to different fields
193	  therefore both survive.

196	- Concurrent edits to one record are serialised with `SELECT ... FOR UPDATE`.
197	- **Exact tie: Salesforce wins.**
198	- **Two portal users, same field (Q4):** both saves apply, and the later one is current. There's no conflict check, so the earlier editor isn't warned. Both edits stay in history.

200	  real edits. *If not:* a later real edit can be ranked as earlier and recorded as superseded.

229	## 10. The 6-hour outage
230	
231	Mechanism: [Architecture](https://github.com/hellosrijan/sync-data/wiki/Architecture), "After an
232	outage".
233	
234	What is on disk:
235	
236	| Item | Where | Note |
237	|---|---|---|
238	| Pending calls (portal edits not yet in Salesforce) | Postgres `outbox` | Committed in the same transaction as the edit. Survive any restart of ours. Never dropped (Q9). |
239	| Portal records and history | Postgres | Not affected by the outage. |
240	| Wake-up signal for the worker | Redis | Losing it loses no work; the outbox still holds every pending call. |
241	| Webhooks missed during the outage | Not on our disk | Recovered by the catch-up query (path C, assumption Q3). |
242	
243	How it drains:
244	
245	1. While Salesforce is down, outbound calls fail and their outbox rows stay pending. A probe
246	   checks every ~10 s whether Salesforce is back.
247	2. On recovery, catch-up runs first: it fetches what changed in Salesforce since the watermark and
248	   applies it. Same-field conflicts are settled before we send anything.
249	3. Then the outbox drains at R = 90 req/s through the shared limiter. Several edits to one record
250	   are merged into one call.
251	
252	Retry policy (Q7, §6): the outbox drives retries at the limiter's pace, and no wait is longer
253	than 60 s. Dramatiq's backoff isn't used, so the drain figures below hold. Recovery is noticed
254	within 10 s (the probe). Catch-up then takes 54 calls, under 1 s, before the drain starts.
255	
256	Arithmetic. Symbols:
257	
258	- T = outage length = 6 h = 21,600 s.
259	- r = portal edits per second during the outage. **Assumed 5/s at peak (Q1).**
260	- R = run rate = **90 req/s (Q10)**, under the cap C = 100.
261	- B = backlog = r × T. One outbound call per edit (merging only makes it smaller).
262	
263	Worst case: the outage covers 6 hours of peak, and new edits keep arriving at r while we drain.
264	
265	- B = 5 × 21,600 = **108,000 calls**
266	- Catch-up first: 108,000 Salesforce-side changes ÷ 2,000 records per query = **54 calls**, < 1 s
267	- Drain: B ÷ (R − r) = 108,000 ÷ (90 − 5) = 108,000 ÷ 85 = **1,271 s ≈ 21.2 min**
268	
269	Other cases, same formula:
270	
271	| Case | r (portal edits/s) | Backlog B | Division | Drain |
272	|---|---|---|---|---|
273	| Worst case: 6 h of peak | 5 | 108,000 | 108,000 ÷ 85 | **21.2 min** |
274	| Normal working day | 1.75 | 37,800 | 37,800 ÷ 88.25 | **7.1 min** |
275	| Overnight (quiet hour) | 0.1 | 2,160 | 2,160 ÷ 89.9 | **24 s** |
276	
277	The r column is the portal share, half of the total in Q1. Working hours average 3.5 edits/s in
278	total, so 1.75 portal; the quiet hour is 0.2 in total, so 0.1 portal.
279	
280	Sensitivity: drain minutes = 21,600 · r ÷ (90 − r) ÷ 60 = 360 · r ÷ (90 − r).
281	
282	| r | Drain after 6 h at that rate |
283	|---|---|
284	| 5 (assumed) | 21 min |
285	| 10 | 45 min |
286	| 20 | 1.7 h |
287	| 45 | 6 h (as long as the outage) |
288	| ≥ 90 | never drains |
289	
290	The design has about 9× headroom over the assumed portal peak before draining takes as long as
291	the outage.
292	
293	Full reconcile, for comparison (Q3): 5,000,000 records ÷ 2,000 per query = 2,500 calls ÷ 90 req/s
